Professional Introduction

As I prepare to transition from nursing curriculum to a registered nurse, my professional identity comes from a place of resilience, always statying curious in the field of nursing, and extreme work ethic. Nursing is such a field that it requires adapting to the environment at all times. I view every clinical challenge as an oppurtunity to expand my judgement and improve my skills. I am very committed to being an lifelong learner and consistently seeking out feedback so that I am able to deliver the highest standard of patient-centered care from day one.

My passion lies in the high-acuity critical care, specifically within the intensive care setting. I am drawn to the complex care of critically ill patients, where rapid assessments, content mastery, and swift intervention all impact survical and recovery. Navigating this fast-paced, high-stakes environment intrigues me and I thrive on the problem solving that is needed to support patients and their families through their most vulnerable moments.

My nursing practice will be anchored by honesty, grit, and a deep commitment to teamwork. In critical care, collaboration is key and I beleive that clear communication, mutual accountability, and humility creates the safest environment for patients. Honesty guides my advocacy and integrity at the bedside, while grit is there so that I remain composed, persistent, and fully present during my shifts as a nurse.

Following graduation, my primary short-term goal is to successfully completely my nurse residency in my CVICU unit position. Over my first year, I aim to master hemodynamic monitoring and really bridge together medication titration, life support technologies while building strong clinical autonomy. Beyond the technical proficiency, I want to be able to establish myself as a dependable, proactive teammate who brings focus and a positive presence to the unit while ultimately preparing for my CCRN certification once I feel competent in my skills.

Reflection

The Gulfgate Windshield Survey and my Emergency Department (ED) Daily Clinical Worksheet demonstrate how I provide safe, competent nursing care, from population-level health equity to individualized bedside care across a patient’s life. In the community setting, my group evaluated census tracts 3115.01, 3115.02, and 3116 around the Harris Health Gulfgate Clinic, where 47.3% of residents are foreign-born, the predominant population is Hispanic, and 44% live below the poverty line with a median household income of $37,955. Our assessment of physical and social determinants of health, such as deteriorated roads, limited full-service grocery stores, aging apartment HVAC units, and the absence of nearby urgent care or behavioral health facilities, showcased clinical competency at a macro system level. Caring for patients in this context means looking beyond individual health behaviors to understand how environmental or community barriers can shape their daily health outcomes and advocating for resources, accessible care, and local partnerships with food and utility services.

In the ED, I was able to use that same holistic awareness and tailor my care across four diverse patients ranging from infancy to middle adulthood. I was able to care for a 13-month-old female presenting with lethargy, respiratory depression, and cannabis toxicity which required rapid clinical judgment. I maintained airway patency and SpO2 above 97% via low-flow oxygen, kept suction ready, administered weight-based acetaminophen, and collaborated with poison control, CPS, and law enforcement. I was able to care for my emotional regulation and professionalism. Although I initially felt frustration toward the infant’s parents, debriefing with a clinical nurse allowed me to process my emotions, remain objective, and provide non-judgmental, supportive communication to the family. Across my other adult patients, I adapted interventions to specific clinical and educational needs – maintaining strict NPO status, large-bore IV access, and hemodynamic stability for a 43-year-old female with active lower gastrointestinal bleeding; performing high-pressure wound irrigation, neurovascular assessments, and tetanus/antibiotic prophylaxis for a 54-year-old female with a dog bite; and conducting an otoscopic exam, pain reassessment to manage acute pain, and self-care teaching for a 35-year-old female with acute otalgia.

These experiences were anchored in the core professional nursing values of human dignity, social justice, integrity, and altruism. Through both artifacts, I learned that equitable, culturally responsive care requires recognizing how language, socioeconomic status, health literacy, and environment influence health, while ensuring personal biases or emotional reactions never compromise patient safety or respect.

Future Growth

As I transition into my career as a registered nurse in the high acuity critical care setting, I will try to integrate macro level social awareness with precise, compassionate bedside manner. In the intensive care unit, physiological stabilization is fast and complex, so I will intentionally prioritize culturally responsive communication, timely interpreter services, and family centered support across every developmental stage. I also plan to build on my reflective practice by utilizing my clinical debriefing during emotionally challenging cases and collaborating early with the interdisciplinary team - including case management and social work to address social determinants of health before patients transition out of critical care settings.

Reflection

Critical thinking and clinical decision-making require looking at subjective and objective cues, anticipating potential risks, and adjusting care when outcomes shift. In my post-operative nursing care plan for an 18-year-old patient recovering from gunshot wounds to the left breast and left arm, ulna fracture repair, and left flexor tendon repair, the most critical clinical information included her wound exposure history (her dressing came off during a 20- to 30-minute altercation, exposing the wound to clothing, room air, and hands, with slight yellow drainage), left-hand motor and strength deficits, and labs showing hemoglobin 9.6, hematocrit 30.6%, potassium 3.4, and urine specific gravity >1.030, paired with 48 hours without a bowel movement and hypoactive bowel sounds.

Using clinical knowledge, my nurse and I focused on infection prevention and monitoring post-surgical and wound integrity as the main priorities alongside addressing opioid-related constipation. Instead of relying on a single intervention, I considered multiple approaches: for bowel elimination, we considered hydration, mobility, bowel regimens, and dietary education while recognizing that low fluid intake, limited activity, and opioid analgesia could stall progress; for wound and post-op care, I performed surgical site inspections and hand hygiene with patient education on warning signs and safe dressing care.

Care coordination and collaboration were central both in care planning and in Integration Lab III. In the care plan evaluation, when the patient had no bowel movement by 1800 and continued reporting constipation with hypoactive bowel sounds and reduced intake and activity, the next step was updating the provider, recommending alternative treatment, and reinforcing hydration and mobility education. In Integration Lab III, we cared for patients across home health scenarios—assessing environmental factors, social determinants of health, and barriers to care—as well as code situations, med-surg units, and mass casualty triage scenes where we assessed and tagged patients. Before each scenario, classmates and I took assigned roles such as primary nurse, bedside nurse, assessment nurse, or medication nurse, then debriefed by role upon completion of the simulation. Each week, upon completion of the simulation, we would review what we did well during the simulation as a group while also addressing our areas for improvement through the weekly workbooks. We would also review content and information that is crucial to our nursing practice. Team communication was always a strength, while areas for improvement often centered on better assessment, acting decisively on specific assessment data, and maintaining strong bedside manner and clinical skills. Evaluating outcomes shaped how I would act in each following scenario.

In the care plan, the short-term bowel goal was not met by 1800, demonstrating why ongoing reassessment and early provider escalation matter when initial measures fall short. In a similar situation, I would escalate unresolved constipation and lab/hydration cues earlier, integrate structured debriefing reflections immediately after high-stress events, and shorten assessment intervals when patient adherence or acuity changes.

Future Growth

As I transition into registered nurse practice, prepare for the NCLEX-RN, and begin my Spring 2027 Nurse Residency Program in Cardiovascular ICU (CVICU), I will sharpen my ability to synthesize rapid clinical data, prioritize life-threatening cues, and collaborate with interprofessional teams under pressure. I will attempt to keep logs of my shifts during my nurse residency program, noting what went well and what areas of improvement I have, while also taking feedback from my peers and interprofessional team members.

Reflection

Evidence-based practice helps turn clinical questions into safer, more responsive patient care. In my EBP presentation comparing mRASS-AW to CIWA-Ar for hospitalized adults experiencing alcohol withdrawal in the emergency department, the clinical question focused on how replacing the subjective, roughly 5-minute, 10-item CIWA-Ar scale with the brief, observational mRASS-AW scale (taking about 30 to 60 seconds) affects hospital length of stay, post-admission complications, and nursing workflow. In my PICO paper on surgical patients with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² compared to ≤ 30 kg/m², I examined airway, ventilation, and other hurdles during anesthesia induction and maintenance.

For evidence and data, my EBP presentation analyzed a Level II, Grade B interrupted time-series study (April 2012 to December 2020) comparing CIWA-Ar and mRASS-AW outcomes, including mean length of stay, post-admission complications, ICU admission rates, delirium and mortality rates, and an estimated 6 to 18 minutes of nursing time saved per patient daily. My PICO paper focused on randomized and clinical trials evaluating McGrath videolaryngoscopy versus direct laryngoscopy, dynamic compliance-guided individualized PEEP versus fixed PEEP, and lean body weight propofol induction dosing for nursing actions.

The evidence directly informed practice changes and patient safety in the clinical application. Switching to mRASS-AW helps introduce faster triage and bedside assessment, particularly during peak withdrawal symptoms or when patients cannot reliably self-report, while combining that with close monitoring for agitation, delirium, and over-sedation. Similarly, the PICO findings supported a specific type of nursing pre-procedure assessment which includes assessing patients for lean body weight dosing, equipment and safety checks, videolaryngoscopy, and individualized PEEP for patients to reduce desaturation, airway difficulty, and postoperative atelectasis while coordinating care to individual patient needs.

Evaluating the study design, sample size, statistical analysis, and limitations taught me that strong evidence combines internal validity with real-world clinical applicability such as recognizing when length-of-stay differences are non-significant despite meaningful workflow and complication insights. In professional practice, I will continuously question routines, appraise research critically, and apply quality-improvement principles to protect patient safety.

Future Growth

As I step into registered nurse practice, prepare for the NCLEX-RN, and begin my Nurse Residency Program, I will use evidence-based inquiry and quality-improvement principles to scrutinize bedside protocols, advocate for safe airway, hemodynamic, and sedation practices, and participate in unit quality initiatives. I will always ground my nursing practice in evidence-based care and research for my interventions and will discover and grow alongside my nursing practice. Grounding my daily critical care decisions in appraised research will allow me to deliver high-reliability, patient-centered care as clinical guidelines advance.
